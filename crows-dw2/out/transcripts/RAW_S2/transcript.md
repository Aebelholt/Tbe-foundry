Orla's mouth flattens at "many times over." She picks the charcoal back up and turns it between her ink-stained fingers, slowly.

"I have heard that sentence from every crow who ever walked out that gate. Some of them even meant it." She does not say it cruelly. "A share of a haul that you have not hauled is a promise written on water. If the vault is as rich as you say, you will not need my two hundred. If it is not, I will be holding nothing."

She looks past you for a moment, toward a shelf at the back of the hall where a row of old tally books sits in a faded line, one slot among them empty.

"Coin I can count. What I cannot count is what the vault might give me that coin can't buy." She drops the charcoal. "So. You have told me what you hope to take out of that place. Ask me what I would want from it."

Orla is quiet long enough that the hall's noise fills the gap, a cart outside, someone hammering. Then she crosses to the back shelf and rests two fingers on the empty slot between the tally books.

"My husband kept the store's accounts for twenty years. His last tally ledger went into that vault with him, the winter he went in after salvage and did not come out. Green cloth cover, a brass clasp, his hand on every page." Her voice stays level, but her fingers do not leave the shelf. "Bring me that book. That is the first thing I want."

She turns back to you.

"The second is plain. Two hundred, repaid in coin at the next cycle, whatever the vault does or does not give you. The coin goes back whether you find a fortune or a corpse's pocket lint. Those are my terms. I will not dress them up."

The charcoal is back in her hand. She is waiting for an answer, and she is not going to fill the silence for you.

Orla looks at your offered hand a moment, as if weighing it against the strongbox. Then she takes it. Her grip is dry and quick, charcoal dust rubbing off on your palm.

"Done." She lets go and wipes her fingers on her cuff, which does nothing for either. "Two hundred gc, counted out at the store counter. Green cloth, brass clasp. And the coin back at the next cycle, book or no book."

She pulls a key from a cord at her neck and weighs it in her hand, then closes her fist around it. For the first time the tiredness in her face gives a little.

"My husband would have liked you well enough. He liked people who asked straight. Go and get your purse before I think better of it."

She turns back to her slate, and the hall's noise closes around you again.
